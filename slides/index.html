Beyond the prompt.

You already know how to ask AI for something and get a decent answer. This session is about the next three steps — and why most companies never take them.

About 30 minutes · No technical background needed · Everything here is written up at github.com/lancelaxa/Your-Augmented-Colleague

Talking points

Open by setting expectations. "Nobody is going to have to write code today. Everything I show you, you could start doing on Monday with the tools you already have."

Ask the room a question to get them engaged: "Hands up — who has used ChatGPT or Claude for work in the last month?" Most hands go up. "Keep them up if you saved that prompt somewhere your colleague could find it." Almost all hands drop. That gap is the entire session.

Set the frame: "You're all good at asking. What we're bad at, as a company, is keeping what works."

Where you are now

Your prompts are fine. That's not the problem.

Most of you already do this well — give it a role, explain the task, paste the material, say what format you want, show an example.

You can alreadyWrite a clear instruction
You can alreadyGet back a usable first draft
ButTomorrow, it's gone

The really good prompt you wrote on Tuesday is buried in your chat history by Thursday. Your colleague works out the same thing from scratch next week. And when a client asks how we produced a report, nobody can show what the AI was actually told.

Takeaway Getting better at asking has a ceiling. Everything after this is about making good work repeatable — so the company keeps it, not just you.

Talking points

The point of this slide: take the pressure off. People assume "learn AI" means "learn to write cleverer prompts." It doesn't. You're telling them their existing skill is fine — so they stop worrying about that and pay attention to what's actually missing.

Make it personal. "Think of the single best prompt you've ever written. Could you find it right now, in under a minute? Could I?" Let that land.

The three failures on the slide, said out loud: you lose it, your colleague duplicates it, and we can't audit it. The third one is the one that matters for government work — if a client or a regulator asks how a document was produced, "someone typed something into ChatGPT" is not an acceptable answer.

The takeaway, in your own words: "The rest of this session isn't about making you better at prompting. It's about making sure that when one of you works something out, all forty of us get it."

Level 02 · The right material

More information isn't better. The right information is.

Everyone's instinct, when the answer is bad, is to paste in more. That usually makes it worse.

What people do

"Summarise this candidate for me."

Then they paste the CV and hope. The summary comes back generic, praises things the client doesn't care about, and reads the same for every candidate.

What works

"Score this candidate against these six criteria. Quote the CV line that supports each score. If the CV doesn't say, write 'not stated'."

Plus the role brief and the scoring sheet. Now every candidate is assessed the same way, and you can compare them side by side.

Same AI. Same candidate. The difference is entirely in what you gave it to work with — the brief, the scoring criteria, and one worked example.

Takeaway Before you ask, give it three things: the standard to measure against, the material to read, and one example of a good answer.

Talking points

This is the highest-value slide for day-to-day work. If people only change one habit, make it this one — and it costs nothing.

Walk through the contrast slowly. Ask: "Who has asked AI to summarise a CV and got back something that sounded fine but told you nothing?" Most hands. "That's not the AI being weak. It's that you never told it what good looks like."

Emphasise 'not stated'. This is the single best trick on the slide. Left to itself, AI fills gaps with confident guesses, because it's built to be helpful. Telling it to flag gaps instead turns an invented detail into a question you can go and check. On a candidate summary, that's the difference between a real assessment and fiction.

Warn them about the opposite failure too: don't dump in everything. If you paste sixty pages, the three lines that matter get lost. Curate — don't hoard.

Practical close: "Where does the scoring sheet live? Right now, in someone's head. That's the problem we fix at level 3."

Level 02 · In practice

Four jobs you do every week.

Write the weekly client update "Summarise the project" Last week's update + what closed this week + the report template

Look at the last column. It's mostly the template, the scoring sheet, last week's version, and a good past example. None of that is clever. It's filing.

Takeaway Most "AI problems" are actually filing problems. Fix where your templates and examples live, and the AI gets better for free.

Talking points

Jargon check — say this out loud: a tender is when a client or a government agency publishes what they need and invites companies to submit a proposal. You'll also hear RFP — Request for Proposal — which is the same idea. In Singapore, most government ones come through GeBIZ, the government's procurement portal. Don't assume everyone in the room knows this; recruiters often don't.

Pick the row that matches your audience and walk through it in detail. Recruiters: the CV row. BD: the tender row. Operations: the weekly update row.

The tender row is worth dwelling on for BD. Asking AI to "summarise this tender" gives you a vague paragraph. Asking it to "list every requirement, quote it exactly, and mark which are compulsory" gives you a checklist you can actually work from — and you can count the rows to check it didn't miss any. That's a genuinely great use of AI: boring, mechanical, and easy to verify.

Land the filing point: "Nobody in this room needs a better AI. You need to know where the current version of the scoring sheet lives."

Level 03 · Loops

Stop pressing enter. Start designing the process.

Prompt-by-prompt supervision stopped scaling with review capacity. Addy Osmani, June 2026 — who named this shift

In plain English: AI can now do far more than any of us has time to check. Checking is the bottleneck — not the AI.

A loop is when the AI does a job, checks its own work against rules you set, fixes what's wrong, and only comes to you when it's done or genuinely stuck. You stop being the person clicking through every step, and become the person who designed the steps.

Takeaway Your scarce resource isn't AI time. It's your attention. A loop spends it only on the exceptions.

Talking points

Establish that this is genuinely new. This idea got its name in June 2026. If you'd searched for it eighteen months ago it didn't exist. Say that — it's why nobody in the room has heard of it, and it's why we're not late.

Translate the quote before moving on. It sounds academic; it isn't. It means: the limit was never how much AI could do. The limit is that a human has to look at all of it. For a team whose whole week is checking other people's work, that should land hard.

Use the junior-hire analogy. "Think about a new consultant. In month one you check everything they do. By month six, you check the tricky ones. What changed? Not their intelligence — you learned what to trust and built a habit around it. A loop is that, written down."

Pre-empt the fear. Somebody is thinking "is this replacing my job?" Address it directly: the loop does the searching, sorting and first-drafting. Every decision that involves money, judgement or a person stays with you. Say it before they ask.

Level 03 · How a loop works

Four parts. Two examples.

Everyday example · doing laundry

Trigger — the basket is full.
The work — wash it.
The check — is it actually clean and dry?
Stop rule — clean → fold it. Still dirty → wash again. Third time still dirty → it's not coming out, deal with it yourself.

You'd never run a machine with no way to tell if the clothes came out clean. That's a loop.

Recruitment example · new CVs

Trigger — every morning at 7am.
The work — take yesterday's applications, remove personal details, score each against the role's scoring sheet.
The check — every criterion scored? every score quotes a CV line? no NRIC or phone number left in?
Stop rule — passes → lands in your shortlist to review. Fails twice → flagged to you by name.

You arrive to a scored shortlist, not forty raw CVs.

Takeaway Trigger, work, check, stop rule. Miss the check and you don't have automation — you have an intern nobody is supervising.

Talking points

Start with the laundry. It gets a laugh and it makes the structure obvious. Everyone instantly agrees you need a way to tell whether the clothes are clean — then point out that most people building AI workflows skip exactly that step.

Then walk the recruitment column slowly. This is the one they'll remember. Emphasise that the consultant still decides who to advance — the loop just did the sorting, the redacting and the first-pass scoring overnight.

Highlight the PII check specifically. "Notice one of the checks is 'no NRIC left in.' That's not a nice-to-have — that's a legal requirement, and a computer is better at spotting it than a tired human at 6pm."

Stress the stop rule. Without it, a stuck AI will keep trying forever, spending money and going nowhere. "Fails twice, it comes to a human" is the whole safety mechanism.

If you have time, ask the room: "What's the equivalent for your desk? What arrives in a pile every morning?" Their answers are your automation shortlist.

Level 03 · The catch

AI aims for "looks finished".

Ask for a client report and you'll get something that looks exactly like a client report. Right length, right headings, confident tone.

Whether the numbers add up, whether the certifications listed are ones we actually hold, whether it answered the question the client asked — those are completely different things, and nothing about "write me a report" makes it get those right.

"Done" is a claim, not a proof.

This is why the check has to come from somewhere other than the thing that did the work. Marking your own homework doesn't work for people either.

Takeaway Confident tone is not evidence of correctness. It's just how the technology writes.

Talking points

This is the trust slide. Everything about safe AI use depends on people internalising it.

Give a concrete horror story. "Imagine a tender response that lists a certification we don't hold. It reads beautifully. It's confident. It's also a misrepresentation to a government agency." Let the room sit with that for a second.

Explain why it happens, so it doesn't sound like AI-bashing: the technology is built to produce plausible, well-formed text. Plausible and correct usually overlap — but when they don't, nothing warns you, because it has no separate sense of "am I right."

The homework line usually lands. "Would you let a consultant review their own placement decision and sign it off? No. Same principle."

Bridge to the next slide: "So the obvious question is — what counts as a good check? Not all checks are equal, and the next slide ranks them."

Level 03 · Ways of checking

Five ways to check work — worst to best.

WORSTIt checks itself "Now review your answer." Like proofreading your own email — you read what you meant to write.
A second AI, same instructions You ask a fresh AI to do the identical task, then compare. Weak, because same instructions and same document tend to produce the same mistake — like two people misreading the same badly-worded sentence.
A second AI, told to attack it Instead of "is this good?" you say "find three things wrong with this." Ask "is this good?" and you'll be told yes. Ask for faults and it finds real ones. Same tool, opposite instruction.
Against a fixed checklist Not an opinion — a scoring sheet. "Are all eight required sections present? Score each 1–5." Same test every time, so results are comparable.
BESTA factual test No judgement at all. Does the pricing column add up? Is the deadline a real future date? Is there an NRIC left in this file? Yes or no.
Takeaway Turn as many checks as possible into yes/no facts. Every check that becomes a fact is one you no longer have to trust anyone's opinion on.

Talking points

Go one rung at a time — this slide rewards slowing down.

Rung 1, self-check: use the email analogy. Everyone has sent an email with a typo they read past five times. You see what you intended, not what's there. AI has the same problem, for the same reason.

Rung 2, second AI same instructions — this is the one they asked about, so explain it properly. "Imagine giving two consultants the identical brief and the identical CV, with no discussion. If the brief is ambiguous, they'll both misread it the same way, and you'll get two matching answers that are both wrong. Two wrong answers that agree feel like confirmation. That's why it's weak."

Rung 3, adversarial — the key point is that the instruction does the work, not the tool. "If you ask me 'is this report good?', I'll be polite and say it's fine. If you ask me 'find me three problems with this report', I'll actually go looking. Identical AI — completely different output. This is free, and almost nobody does it."

Tell them to steal rung 3 today. Paste your draft into a fresh chat with "find three things wrong with this." Fresh chat matters — in the same conversation it's still marking its own homework.

Rung 5: "Does this column add up" has no opinion in it. It's right or it's wrong. Those are the checks worth building first because they never get tired and never get talked round.

Level 03 · Why checking matters this much

Small mistakes multiply. Fast.

Say the AI gets each step right 95% of the time. Sounds excellent — that's an A grade.

But steps stack. Step 2 works from step 1's answer. If step 1 was wrong, step 2 is wrong too, no matter how well it performs.

If each step is right…after 3 stepsafter 10 stepsafter 20 steps
70% of the time34%3%almost never
90% of the time73%35%12%
95% of the time86%60%36%
99% of the time97%90%82%

Read the highlighted row: 95% right at every single step still only gets you 60% right over a ten-step job.

Takeaway This is just arithmetic — it happens to people too. It's why we check at each stage instead of only at the end.

Talking points

This is the most important slide in the deck. Slow right down.

Build it with a human example first, before showing the numbers. "A CV gets typed into the system with the wrong notice period. Nobody catches it. It flows into the shortlist, into the client submission, into the interview scheduling, into the offer. By the time anyone notices, five things are wrong — from one small mistake at the start." Everyone in the room has lived this.

Then read the highlighted row aloud, slowly. "Ninety-five percent accurate at every single step. Ten steps. Sixty percent." Pause. "That's a coin flip on a job you thought was an A grade."

Head off the wrong conclusion. Someone will say "so AI isn't reliable enough." Wrong lesson. The right lesson is three things: use fewer steps, check at each stage rather than only at the end, and put a human at the important points. Say those three explicitly.

If someone asks whether this is really about AI: no, and that's the point. It applies to any multi-step process, including entirely human ones. It's why your existing sign-off gates exist. AI just runs the steps faster, so it hits you sooner.

If the maths people want it: it's 0.95 multiplied by itself ten times. Don't dwell there — most of the room doesn't need it.

Level 03 · The most useful habit today

If it keeps getting it wrong — start a new chat.

Yes. Genuinely. Don't keep arguing with it in the same conversation.

What everyone does

"That's not right, try again."
"Still wrong, try again."
"No, I said quarterly…"

Every failed attempt is still sitting in the conversation. The AI reads its own wrong answers as background and keeps walking down the same path. Researchers measured it: after a failure, the next attempt is about seven times more likely to fail too.

What to do instead

1. Open a brand-new chat.
2. Paste the original request again.
3. Add one line: "An earlier attempt confused quarterly with monthly figures — be careful with that."
4. Don't paste the failed answer in.

You keep the lesson. You throw away the confusion.

Takeaway Two tries in the same chat. On the third, start fresh. It's the cheapest habit on this slide deck and it works immediately.

Talking points

Answer the question directly, because it's the one people actually have: yes — if you're going round in circles, start again in a clean chat. Say it plainly.

Explain why, because the reason makes it stick. A chat is one long document, and the AI re-reads the whole thing every time. When it fails four times, those four wrong answers are still on the page. It's now reading its own mistakes as if they were reference material. You've made the problem worse by trying harder.

Use a human analogy. "It's like someone who's talked themselves into a corner. Sometimes you don't argue them out of it — you change the subject, come back tomorrow, and start clean."

The one thing to carry across: a single sentence about what went wrong. Not the failed output — just the lesson. That's the whole trick.

Give them the rule of thumb and repeat it: two tries, then fresh chat. It's concrete, it's memorable, and they can use it this afternoon.

Level 03 · Why these projects fail

It's not that AI isn't clever enough. It loses track.

60%
of AI failures in real companies come from the system losing track of what's already happened — not from bad reasoning.
A week on a live role

Monday — you ask AI to help manage a 12-person shortlist. It scores them all. Good.

Wednesday — two candidates withdraw. You mention it in passing, in a different chat.

Thursday — the client drops one requirement and adds another. You update your notes.

Friday — you ask for a revised shortlist. It gives you a confident, well-written list that includes a withdrawn candidate and scores everyone against Monday's requirements.

Nothing here was a reasoning failure. It simply never knew about Wednesday or Thursday.

Takeaway Keep one living file that everyone and everything reads from — the tracker, not the chat thread. Chats forget. Files don't.

Talking points

This is the "why do these projects fail" slide, and the example does the heavy lifting. Walk through the week day by day — don't rush it.

Land the key point at the end: "Every single thing it did was reasonable, based on what it knew. The problem was what it didn't know. It's not a stupidity problem, it's a bookkeeping problem." Then point at the 60%.

Make it feel familiar, because it is. This is the same failure as working from last month's version of the rate card, or two people editing different copies of the same tender. We already know this failure — AI just does it faster and with more confidence.

Give them the fix concretely: one tracker per role or per project. When something changes, it changes there. Every time you ask AI for help, that file is what you give it — not your memory of a conversation.

Connect it to the repo: this is exactly why the trackers and playbooks are in a shared, version-controlled place rather than in someone's inbox. Same fix, bigger scale.

Level 04 · Graphs — the simplest possible version

A loop is one person. A graph is the whole flowchart.

A loop

One person, one job, doing it over and over until it's right.

"Score these CVs until they're all scored properly."

A graph

Several people, each doing their bit, in an order — with rules about who does what next and who has to sign off.

"BD writes it → compliance checks it → finance prices it → if it's over S$500k the partner approves → then it goes out."

You already have graphs. You just draw them on whiteboards instead of calling them that.

Your tender approval process is a graph. Your hiring pipeline is a graph. Your milestone sign-off is a graph. "Graph engineering" just means drawing that flowchart precisely enough that software can follow it — and putting the approvals in the right places.

Takeaway Nothing new to learn conceptually. It's your existing approval process, written down properly.

Talking points

This is the explain-it-simply slide. Do not use technical language anywhere on it.

The one-line version, if you say nothing else: "A loop is one person doing one job well. A graph is a team with a flowchart."

Take the mystery out immediately. Ask: "Has anyone here ever drawn a process on a whiteboard with boxes and arrows?" Hands go up. "Congratulations, you've done graph engineering. The only new thing is drawing it precisely enough that software can follow it."

Use their own process as the example. For BD: enquiry comes in, someone qualifies it, someone decides bid or no-bid, someone writes it, someone checks compliance, someone approves the price, someone submits. That's seven boxes and six arrows. That's a graph.

Head off the anxiety. Nobody in this room is going to build one. What you need is the vocabulary — so when a vendor pitches an "agentic workflow," you can ask sensible questions. There's a slide on exactly which questions later.

Level 04 · The three pieces

Case file, desks, handover rules.

The case file Everything known so far, travelling with the job. The tender reference, what's been drafted, what compliance said, who approved the price. Every desk reads it and adds to it.
The desks Each person or step doing one thing. BD drafts. Compliance checks. Finance prices. One job each.
The handover rules What happens next, and when. "Under S$100k, the manager signs. Over S$500k, the partner and finance sign."

A well-run process keeps the folder with the job. A badly-run one loses it — someone works from an old version, two people make contradictory decisions, and afterwards nobody can reconstruct who approved what.

That's the same 60% failure from earlier, in human form. The fix is identical: one file, added to rather than overwritten, with a history you can read back.

Takeaway If you can't say who approves what at each value threshold, don't automate anything yet. You'd just be automating the confusion.

Talking points

The case file is the idea that matters — spend your time there. Everyone understands a physical folder travelling between desks with everything in it. That's the whole concept.

For BD in Singapore, use a real shape. A GeBIZ tender: the reference number, the closing date, the compulsory requirements, our draft answers, the compliance sign-off, the approved pricing, who authorised submission. All of it travelling together. If any piece goes missing, someone downstream makes a decision on incomplete information.

Make the handover rules concrete. Ask the room: "Right now, who signs off a S$300,000 bid?" If you get three different answers or a pause, that is the finding. Say so.

The takeaway is a genuine warning, not a slogan. Automating an unclear approval process makes the confusion faster and harder to see. Writing the process down is valuable on its own — even if we never automate a single step.

Level 04 · The mistake that costs the most

Approve before, not find out after.

Approve before ✓

"May I submit this?"

The process stops and waits — for an hour or a week — then carries on exactly where it left off once you say yes. Nothing is lost by waiting.

Use this for anything you can't take back: submitting a tender, emailing a client, sending a candidate's details, transmitting a deliverable.

Notify after ✗

"I've submitted this."

It already happened. You're reading a notification, not making a decision.

This is how something everyone calls "the approval step" turns out to be an announcement — usually discovered at the worst possible moment.

When someone shows you an automated process, this is the first question worth asking: "Does it stop and wait for me, or does it tell me afterwards?"

Takeaway One irreversible step, one human saying yes first. Bids, client emails, candidate data — no exceptions.

Talking points

This is the slide with the clearest financial consequence — make it vivid.

Give the scenario: "Picture a system that assembles our tender response and submits it to GeBIZ at the deadline. Someone tells you there's an approval step. But it emails the partner as it submits. That's not approval — that's a receipt. And the bid is gone."

Give them the question to take away. Say it twice: "Does it stop and wait for me, or does it tell me afterwards?" That single question separates a real approval process from a fake one, and they don't need any technical knowledge to ask it.

Mention the free benefit. Because the system records every step and every approval as it goes, you get the audit trail automatically — who approved what, when, based on what information. For government work that's not a bonus, it's often a requirement, and here you get it without anyone maintaining a separate spreadsheet.

Tie back to slide 11: a human approval also resets the error build-up. Once you've verified it, everything downstream starts from a clean, checked position.

Level 04 · Worked example for BD

Monday morning tender scanning.

Today

Every Monday, someone spends three hours going through GeBIZ and agency portals, opening each new tender, working out whether it's worth bidding, and writing it up for the Tuesday pipeline meeting.

It's dull, it's repetitive, and when that person is on leave, it simply doesn't happen.

As a loop

6am Monday — it checks what's new since last week.
Filters — against what we can actually deliver.
Summarises — scope, value, closing date, compulsory requirements.
Checks — every field filled? closing date real and in the future? link works?
Then stops and hands you the list.

You arrive Monday to twenty minutes of triage on a filtered, summarised list.

What is deliberately not automated: the decision to bid. That's judgement, money and relationships. The loop does the searching and the summarising. You still decide — you just stopped being the search engine.

Takeaway Automate the finding and the checking. Never the deciding.

Talking points

This is the payoff slide for BD — make the current pain real first. Ask whoever does the Monday scan how long it takes and what happens when they're away. Let them answer. The honest answer is usually "it doesn't get done", and that's a live commercial risk: a missed tender is a missed bid.

Walk through the checks and point out they're all factual. Is the field filled in? Is the closing date a real date in the future? Does the link work? No opinions anywhere — which is why this is a good, safe first automation.

Emphasise the boundary hard. The loop never decides whether to bid. Say it clearly, because this is where people get nervous: "It hands you a shortlist. You decide. What it removes is three hours of clicking through portals."

Point out the fairness angle: the loop runs whether or not someone is on leave, sick, or busy with a live bid. Consistency is the underrated benefit.

Ask the room for their version. "What's the equivalent three hours on your desk?" Write the answers down — that's your genuine starting backlog.

Where we actually are

Six levels. We're on the first one.

LVL 0Chatting You type, read, copy, paste. ← almost all of us, today
LVL 1Saved prompts The good ones written down where everyone can find them. Free — no technology needed
LVL 2Written checklist The steps written out, plus what "done properly" means. Free
LVL 3AI does it, you check everything Still fully supervised.
LVL 4AI does it and checks itself You see the exceptions, plus a random sample.
LVL 5It runs on schedule Starts itself, remembers, escalates when stuck.

Don't jump to the end. Level 2 is where you find out what your checklist actually needs to catch. Skip it and you build something that confidently checks the wrong things.

Takeaway Levels 1 and 2 need no budget, no software and no permission. That's most of the value, available this week.

Talking points

Be honest about where we are. Level 0 isn't a criticism — it's where nearly every company is. But say plainly that staying there is a choice.

Push hard on levels 1 and 2 being free. No purchase, no IT project, no approval needed. It's writing things down. If leadership is in the room, this is the low-risk ask: give people time to document what they already do.

Explain why skipping to level 5 fails. "If you automate a process you've never written down, you're guessing at what to check. You'll build something that passes everything and catches nothing." The two weeks of manual checklist use is where you discover the real failure modes.

Set a realistic target. Suggest: every team at level 2 on one process within a month. That's achievable and it's genuinely useful on its own — even if we stop there.

Talking points

Don't rush this because it's near the end. For a firm handling candidate data and government work, it's the slide with real consequences.

Be absolutely unambiguous on NRIC. There is never a good reason for an NRIC to be in a prompt. Screening, summarising, matching, ranking — none of them need an identity number. If one's in there, something went wrong upstream.

Give the regulator test and encourage people to actually use it. It resolves most edge cases faster than reading a policy document.

Make reporting safe, explicitly. Say this out loud: "If you paste something you shouldn't have, tell your manager and the DPO the same day. Reporting a mistake is never a disciplinary matter. Hiding one is." People only report if they believe that, and near-misses are how we fix the process.

Flip it commercially for the BD audience. Being able to show how we govern AI use is becoming a scored part of public-sector bids. Good governance isn't overhead here — it's increasingly the reason we're allowed to compete.

Six things, in order.

01Read the rulesStart Here and Compliance. Half an hour.
02Write one process downSomething you do every week. Numbered steps.
03Write what "done" meansThe checklist. Which items are simple yes/no facts?
04Share your best promptPut it where the team can find it.
05Use your own checklistTwo weeks. Write down every time it fails.
06Then think about automatingAnd automate the checking before the writing.

The most valuable thing we could build first isn't a writing assistant. It's a personal-data detector — a simple check that spots an NRIC or a phone number before anything is sent anywhere. Boring, entirely factual, and it protects everything else we build afterwards.

Takeaway Steps 1 to 5 cost nothing but attention. Nobody needs permission to start them.

Talking points

Make step 5 the one you emphasise. Using your own checklist manually for two weeks feels like a delay. It isn't — it's how you find out what the checklist is missing. Every failure you write down becomes a check worth building.

Explain "check before write" once more, since it's counter-intuitive. Everyone wants AI to write things. But a bad draft is obvious and gets fixed. A missed check is silent — a pricing table that doesn't add up, a missing compulsory section, an NRIC left in a file. Those are the ones that actually cost us.

Ask for a named volunteer per team. One process, one person, written up within a month. Vague commitments produce nothing; a named person and a date produce something.

Point to the repository as the place it all goes. Not someone's inbox, not a personal drive — the shared, versioned place where everyone can find it and changes get reviewed.

If you remember one thing

Not "can AI do this?"
"Would I know if it got it wrong?"

If you can't write down what "done properly" looks like, you're not ready to automate it — you'd just be creating work nobody is checking.
