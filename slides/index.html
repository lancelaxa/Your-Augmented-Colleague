Beyond the prompt.

The wallNothing you learned survives the conversation

The good prompt you wrote on Tuesday is gone by Thursday. Your colleague solves the same problem from scratch next week. Nobody can check what the AI was told before it produced the report that went to a client.

That is not a prompting problem. Better wording will not fix it.

The ladder

Four rungs. They stack, they don’t replace.

01 PROMPTOne message What do I say?
You control the wording.
02 CONTEXTThe whole window What should it see?
You control the information.
03 LOOPOne agent’s cycle How does it run without me — and when does it stop?
04 GRAPHMany agents Who does what, in what order, with which approvals?

A graph is made of loops. A loop is made of prompts running against a context. Getting good at rung 3 does not let you skip rung 1 — a bad prompt inside an automated loop is just a bad prompt that now runs 200 times unsupervised.

Rung 02 · Context engineering

The skill is curation, not accumulation.

Everyone’s instinct on a bad answer is to paste in more. That makes it worse — and the failure has a name.

Distraction60 pages of tender boilerplate bury the 3 evaluation criteria
Clashingv3 and v7 of the pricing sheet are both in the folder
PoisoningTurn 2 misreads the deadline. Turns 3–40 build on it.

Context rot: as the window fills, the model’s ability to attend to what matters degrades. Bigger context windows did not solve this.

Four moves — WRITE it outside the window · SELECT only what this step needs · COMPRESS finished work · ISOLATE into separate windows.
Rung 02 · In practice

What “good context” looks like here.

TaskWeakStrong
Screen a CV The CV alone Anonymised CV + role brief + scoring rubric + one worked example
Draft a tender response “Write an RFP response” Evaluation criteria + our 3 best past responses + win themes + page limit
Weekly status report “Summarise the project” Milestone tracker + last week’s report + closed issues + the template
Summarise a policy doc The 80-page PDF The 6 pages that changed + last version’s summary + “what changed?”

The pattern in the right column: the rubric, the template, the prior example, the delta. Three of those four are files that should live in a shared repository.

This is where AI literacy and “learn GitHub” become the same project. The repo is not where you store the work — the repo is the context.
Rung 03 · Loop engineering

Named in June 2026. Not a buzzword — a dated shift.

Developers shouldn’t be prompting coding agents anymore. They should be designing loops that prompt your agents. Peter Steinberger — the line that named it
Prompt-by-prompt supervision stopped scaling with review capacity. Addy Osmani — on why the shift happened

Read that second one again. It is the business case in one sentence.

The bottleneck was never that AI couldn’t do more. It is that a human has to look at everything it does, and there are only so many hours. For a team whose week is spent checking other people’s work, that is not a technical curiosity — it is the job description.

Rung 03 · Anatomy

Four parts. Miss one and you have a liability.

01 TRIGGERWhat starts it A schedule, an event, a queue. Without one you don’t have a loop — you have a task you must remember to run.
02 TOPOLOGYWhat it does The work itself. Your prompt and context skills, unchanged.
03 VERIFIERThe independent check Something other than the doer decides whether it is actually done.
04 STOP RULEThe boundary Pass, budget exhausted, or escalate to a human. You need all three.
A loop with no verifier is not automation. It is an unsupervised intern with your client’s letterhead.
Rung 03 · The hard part

Agents aim for “looks done”.

Ask for a tender response and you get something shaped exactly like a tender response. Right length, right headings, confident tone.

Whether the pricing table reconciles, whether the certifications cited are ones we actually hold, whether it answers the question asked — those are different properties. Nothing in “generate a document” optimises for them.

The whole reason you split the verifier from the maker is to make the loop’s “it’s done” mean something. And even then, “done” is a claim, not a proof.
Which is why high-stakes output still needs a human gate — see rung 04.
Rung 03 · Verifier strength

Push every check as far down this list as you can.

★☆☆☆☆Self-check “Now check your answer.” Very low trust — it already thinks it’s right.
★★☆☆☆Second agent, same prompt Correlated blind spots.
★★★☆☆Adversarial prompt “Find three things wrong with this.” Framing does real work.
★★★★☆Rubric-based Score against a fixed written checklist. Reproducible, auditable.
★★★★★Mechanical Does the pricing table sum? Is every mandatory section present? Not an opinion — a fact.

Checks that are already mechanical in our work, and nobody is using them: every mandatory RFP section present · quoted rates match the approved card · any NRIC-shaped string in a CV summary · every named certification on our verified list.

Rung 03 · The constraint

Reliability compounds downward.

If each step succeeds with probability p, an N-step run succeeds with pN. This is arithmetic, not pessimism.

Per-step accuracy3 steps51020100

A 95%-accurate agent is a 60%-accurate workflow after ten steps. The fix is not a better model. It is fewer steps, mechanical checks, and human checkpoints.

Rung 03 · Two consequences

Retrying wrong makes it worse. Gates make it better.

The retry trap
7.1×

Failed attempts raise the per-step error rate by 7.1× over baseline — because the failed attempt stays in the conversation and contaminates the next one. Rutgers, May 2026

So “just try again” is actively harmful. Retry with a clean context: reset the window, keep the task plus a short note on what failed.

The reset
↺

Once a human confirms an output, the accumulated failure risk resets — the rest of the chain starts fresh from a verified state.

A human gate is not friction. It is a mathematical reset on compounding error. Place them before anything irreversible, client-facing, contractual, or touching personal data.

Rung 04 · Graph engineering

Loops are one agent. Graphs are the org chart.

A loop breaks the moment the path has to branch, route between specialists, or survive a human in the middle.

StateThe case file that travels with the job. Every desk reads it, adds to it, passes it on.
NodesThe desks. One person or team doing one thing.
EdgesThe handover rules. “If value > S$500k, route to partner.”

You draw the steps and connections ahead of time, and let the model fill in the boxes. You trade some autonomy for something operations teams value far more: predictability, auditability, and the ability to put a gate anywhere.

You already understand graphs. An org chart is a graph. An approval workflow is a graph. A tender process with stage gates is a graph.
Rung 04 · The detail that catches everyone

Approve before, or find out after.

interrupt_before

“May I send this to the client?”

The process pauses, persists its state, and waits — for a minute or a week — then resumes exactly where it stopped, with full context intact.

Use this for anything irreversible: submitting a bid, emailing a client, publishing a report, transmitting personal data.

interrupt_after

“I have sent this to the client.”

The action has already happened before the pause.

Getting this backwards is how an “approval workflow” turns out to be a notification workflow — discovered at the worst possible moment.

Bonus: every state change gets checkpointed, so you can inspect, rewind and fork any point — like git commits. The audit trail becomes a by-product of the architecture rather than a documentation chore.
Worked example

The Monday morning tender loop.

Today

Every Monday a BD manager spends three hours checking GeBIZ and agency portals, opening each tender, deciding whether it fits, and writing a summary for the Tuesday pipeline meeting.

It is dull, repetitive, and when they are on leave it does not happen.

As a loop

Trigger — Monday 06:00.
Topology — scan, filter against our capability profile, summarise.
Verifier — all fields populated? deadline a real future date? source URL resolves? PII scan clean? All mechanical.
Stop — pass → draft an issue per tender; 2 failed retries → escalate.

Monday becomes twenty minutes of triage on a pre-filtered list.

Deliberately not automated: the bid/no-bid decision. That is judgement, money and relationships. The loop prepares the decision — the human makes it. They just stopped being the search engine.
Where we actually are

Climb it in order. Don’t jump to 5.

LVL 0Manual chat You prompt, read, copy-paste. ← most of us are here
LVL 1Saved prompts A shared, versioned prompt library. Free — no technology needed
LVL 2Checklisted run Documented sequence + written verification checklist. Free
LVL 3Assisted loop Agent runs it; human verifies every output.
LVL 4Verified loop Automated verifier gates. Human sees exceptions plus samples.
LVL 5Scheduled loop Triggers itself, keeps state, escalates when stuck.

Level 2 is where you discover what your verifier needs to check. Teams that skip it build loops that confidently verify the wrong things. Write the checklist by hand, use it for a fortnight, then automate it.

Non-negotiable

Before any of this touches real data.

Never, in any tool NRIC / FIN · passport numbers · candidate phone, email, address · dates of birth · medical information · client-confidential terms in a consumer account
Always Anonymise before the prompt, not after the output · approved tools only · human review before anything leaves CGP

Monday morning

Six things, in order.

01ReadStart Here + Compliance. 30 minutes.
02Write one process downSomething you repeat weekly, as numbered steps.
03Write its checklistWhat does “done correctly” mean? Which checks are mechanical?
04Share your best promptInto the shared library, via a pull request.
05Run it manuallyTwo weeks against your own checklist. Note every failure.
06Only then automateAnd automate the checking before the drafting.

The most valuable first automation is not a drafting assistant. It is a PII detector — fully mechanical, addresses the highest-risk failure in the business, and protects everything built after it.

The one question

Not “can this be automated?”
“Can this be verified?”

If you cannot define “done correctly” in writing, you do not have an automation candidate. You have an unaudited liability waiting to happen.
